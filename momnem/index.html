The brief I gave myself

Mom 'n 'em announced a latte art throwdown on Facebook with a Fellow Series 1 espresso machine as the grand prize, a $15 competitor entry, a raffle, smashburgers and a DJ.

Everything in the email comes from that post. Nobody asked for this.

One email is an announcement. Three is a campaign.

Mon 15th
Latte art throwdown, Friday the 25th
Announce. Full detail, calendar add.
Tue 22nd
Three days out: burgers, wine, and a bracket
Remind. Leads with the food, not the coffee.
Fri 25th
Tonight at 5:30 in Madisonville
Show up. Two sentences and directions.

The second email leads with smashburgers and wine because the list is mostly not baristas. A bracket is the draw for a tenth of them. Giving up a Friday night needs a different reason.

Decisions worth defending

The prize leads, not the competition. A grand-prize espresso machine is the hook for everyone who does not care about latte art, which is most of the list. The bracket is the draw for maybe a tenth of them.

The artwork carries the date and address, so the alt text has to. Baking them into the maroon block gives the brand its own display type, which live HTML cannot do. The cost is that a reader with images off gets nothing, so the full date, time and street address sit in the image’s alt attribute. It is a trade, and it is the one place in this campaign where design won over resilience.

Competing and the raffle are quiet. Small audiences, paid actions. Making them loud would invert the hierarchy against the free primary CTA, which is what the other 95% of the list is deciding about.

Only the announce gets a shop link. Most of a coffee shop’s list will not give up a Friday, so the first send ends with somewhere for them to go. The reminder and the day-of deliberately do not repeat it. Their job is attendance, and a second call to action on a day-of email is a leak.

The raffle is the strongest mechanic in the event. “You have to be there to win” makes a $10 ticket a firmer commitment to attend than any calendar add. That is why it gets a block rather than a line.

Each send assumes the reader never opened the last one. Date, time and address repeat in all three. What changes is the lead, because the question changes: what is this, then am I giving up a Friday, then what am I doing tonight.

What I measured

Opinions are cheap in a portfolio. These are numbers off the built files.

Every primary CTA lands above the mobile fold, which sits near 740px on an iPhone after mail-app chrome. Email 2 first put its calendar button at 1,142px because the artwork sat between the copy and the ask. Moving the artwork below the CTA and tightening four paddings brought it to 703.

Length falls as urgency rises. 3,532 to 1,663 to 1,242. By the third email a reader can tell at a glance that this one is quick, which is what you want at 3pm on a Friday.

The CTA changes with the clock. Add-to-calendar on the announce and the reminder, directions on the day-of. A calendar add at 3pm on the day is worthless.

Every image is under 400KB and each asset URL carries a content hash, so a replaced file cannot serve stale from a CDN or a browser cache.
